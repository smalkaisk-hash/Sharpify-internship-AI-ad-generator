0. Read this first
This audience cannot be sold to. They have been sold to by every
wealth-management firm, private bank, yacht broker, and luxury
concierge service for thirty years. They are immune. The only register
that converts is the register of an equal speaking quietly to another
equal.
Every editorial decision in this film answers one question: would a
sixty-year-old with twenty million in liquid assets, sitting on his sofa in
Monaco with his wife next to him, watch this without rolling his eyes?
If you cannot answer yes for a specific cut, the cut is wrong. Fix it or
remove it.

1. Conversion targets
This brief is operational, not artistic. Every editorial choice is judged
against the numbers below but read this section carefully: the targets
are starting hypotheses, not benchmarks. Set true targets against the
client's prior funnel data within the first 14 days of paid traffic, then

UHNW cold traffic to a 4-minute-plus VSL behaves differently from
typical direct-response audiences.
2. Who you are editing for
You are not editing for a generic luxury audience. You are editing for one
specific human, sitting on a specific sofa, in a specific moment.
He is sixty-two. He built or grew real businesses. He has homes in two of
{Monaco, Geneva, Lugano, Milan, Como, Dubai}. His wife is on the sofa
next to him when he opens this on his iPad. She decided to click play.
Her opinion will determine whether they fill out the application and
whether they ever talk about it again.
He is sceptical. Three of his friends have been disappointed by Tiger 21
or YPO chapters. He has a daughter who is eighteen and already
strategic, and he is starting to feel quietly that he does not know how to
talk to her about what he is going to leave behind.
Edit for him. Edit for her sitting beside him. Every cut decision is made
with both of them in the frame of your imagination.
3. The reference
One reference. Anchor here, ignore the rest.
Errol Morris's Interrotron interview Errol Morris on Donald Rumsfeld:
'One of the strangest interviews I've ever done' style the way subjects
look directly into the lens and speak as if to one specific person, without
performance, without rhetorical structure, with the camera as a witness
rather than a stage.
That is the §4b paired beat. That is the entire visual language of the film.
The camera forgets it is a camera. The talent forgets they are on
camera. The viewer feels they are being addressed by name.

What we are not referencing: luxury commercial work, watch-ad

4. The 8-second hook audit
The first 8 seconds carry the largest single share of viewer-decision
weight in any VSL Wistia engagement data places the typical decision
window at 5–8 seconds. Before any other edit decision, the post lead
must audit §1 against this test:
• At second 8, does the viewer understand the stakes? (Family
wealth disappearing across generations.)
• At second 8, does the viewer trust the speaker? (Calm, low
register, no sales tone.)
• At second 8, is the viewer mildly uncomfortable? (Good. Comfort
means we are not landing.)
If any of the three answers is no, recut §1 before continuing with the rest
of the film. Do not proceed to §2 edits until §1 passes audit.
5. Three protected mechanics
These are conversion mechanics dressed as direction notes. Cutting any
of them costs applications. They are not stylistic preferences.
5.1 The two-second pause before 'I will tell you the reason this is
personal.'
After Jonas says 'That is the pattern,' there must be two full seconds of
silence on his face before the next line. If the recorded pause is shorter,
extend it with room tone. If you find this pause uncomfortably long on
your monitor, you have it the right length. This pause is what tells the
viewer the next thing is true.
5.2 No eye contact between Jonas and Camelia in §4b and §8.
They are seated together but each looks at the lens, not at each other. If
a take has them looking at each other, do not use it. If only that take
exists for a line, cut to a single before the eye contact happens. Couples

5.3 Camelia's line is the line.

8. Visual grammar
Color. Single muted palette throughout — cream, warm neutral, deep
burgundy as accent only. The grade must be invisible to a non-editor. If
the look is visible, the look is wrong. Match the Project Dynasty deck
palette.
Light. Single soft key, slightly above eye-line, falling off into shadow on
the unlit side. Do not flatten with fill. Shadows are part of the register.
Framing. Faces. Tight enough that the eyes read. Loose enough that
body language reads in §4b. No Dutch tilts, no symmetry games, no
visible composition tricks.
Movement. Static throughout. Even §6 walking shot is observational, not
kinetic. No push-ins on emotional beats.

-- 6 of 19 --

Cuts. Slow. The instinct from social-edit training will be cuts every 2–3
seconds. Fight it. Four-second holds on faces are not dead time. They
are the line landing.
9. Sound
Music. Silence under §1 and §4. Restrained ambient strings under §2,
§3, §6, §7, §8 only — well beneath the dialogue floor. Reference: Max
Richter's quieter work, Nils Frahm solo piano. Nothing with rhythm.
Nothing with crescendo.
Voice. Warm and forward. Light de-essing only. Trust the recorded
intimacy. Audible breaths stay in.
Room tone. Use generously. Two-second pauses with room tone read as
truthful silence. The same pauses with digital silence read as edit error.
The film ends in silence, not in music. Music fades with the image to
black. No sting on the CTA card.

Two on-screen elements only. Anything beyond these is a kill-list
violation.

Two variants ship at launch. The losing variant is killed at day 14.

1. The film, in one paragraph
This is not a podcast and not a corporate piece. It is a four-and-a-half-minute filmed
confession from two co-founders to ultra-high-net-worth families. The audience is wealthy,
sceptical, and surrounded by people trying to sell them something. They have seen every
glossy club video. The only register that works on them is restraint. Quiet camera, quiet light,

two people who sound like they mean what they are saying. Anything that feels staged loses
the room in the first ten seconds.
2. Talent and energy
Jonas carries seven of eight sections alone. He is the lead voice. European register, around
130 words per minute, with real pauses. He should sound like he is at dinner with a peer, not
presenting from a stage. Contractions are minimal. Vulnerability appears once — in §4 —
and it must not be rushed.
Camelia appears in §4b and §8. Her presence is structural, not decorative. She is the person
who first noticed the gap Jonas talks about. She must sound like a co-diagnostician, not a
supporting wife. Her Section 4 line — 'I watched Jonas with him at the dinner table, and I
saw something I had seen in my own father' — is the most important line either of them
speaks in the entire video. If a take of that line lands, every other take of it becomes
secondary footage. Get it on take one or two while she is still finding the words.
3. Two non-negotiable rules
3.1 The pause before 'I will tell you the reason this is
personal.'
In §4, after Jonas delivers the Brooks Newmark story and says 'That is the pattern,' he
pauses for two full seconds before the next line. Two seconds feels like an eternity on
camera. Do not let him cut it short. If he rushes it, reset and do another take. This pause is
what tells the viewer the next thing they hear is true.
3.2 No eye contact in the paired shots (§4b and §8).
Jonas and Camelia are seated together but they do not look at each other while they speak.
Each of them looks at the lens. They do not nod while the other is speaking. They do not
reach for each other. They do not smile reassuringly between lines.
This rule will feel wrong to them. Couples performing intimacy for an audience always read
as theatre. Two people independently telling the same truth to the camera read as marriage.
Brief them on this before they sit down. Block it on rehearsal. If they slip, reset.

7. Hands and stillness
This piece lives or dies on restraint. The single fastest way to break the register is to let the
talent gesture like they are presenting from a stage. Hands need their own direction,
separate from the rest of the body.
4.1 The default is stillness.
In §1, §3, §4a, §4b, §5, §7 and §8, the default state for both pairs of hands is still. Resting on
the lap or on the chair arms. Out of frame whenever the lens allows. The audience reads still
hands as 'I mean what I am saying.' They read moving hands as 'I am performing.'
4.2 Section 2 is the only exception.
§2 has three numbered points first, second, third. Jonas can lift one hand for one beat per
step, palm slightly turned toward camera, and let it return to rest. That is three small lifts
across the section. No counting on fingers. Counting on fingers is a TED-talk move and
reads as amateur. The hand suggests the enumeration; the words carry it.
4.3 The §4b paired beat is where most crews fail.
Sitting beside each other, talking about their family, Jonas and Camelia will instinctively
reach for each other. A hand on the knee. A hand on the forearm. A small reassuring
squeeze between lines. This destroys the section.
The whole reason §4b works is that they are independently telling the truth, not performing
tenderness for the camera. Touching breaks the spell. Brief them clearly before they sit
down: hands stay on their own lap, no exceptions, and no comforting gestures between
lines. If they slip, reset and roll again. They will not understand why this rule exists until they
see the cut.

-- 17 of 19 --

4.5 The reset hand.
Between takes, Jonas's natural tendency is to bring his hands to his face —adjusting his
collar, pushing hair back, rubbing his jaw. This is fine off-camera, but it leaks into the start of
the next take if he doesn't drop his hands fully before 'action.' The DP needs to call hand
reset, not just talent reset, before each roll. A take that begins with a hand still drifting down
from the face starts late and feels unstable.
4.6 Things to actively avoid.
• Steepled fingers. Reads as politician, not co-founder.
• Open palms held forward for emphasis. Stage register, wrong audience.
• Counting on fingers during §2.
• Pointing at the camera. Ever.
• Hand-on-heart gestures during §4. The vulnerability is in the words. Adding hands to it
